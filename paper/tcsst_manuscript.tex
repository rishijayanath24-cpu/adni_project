\section*{Abstract}
Adaptive modality-acquisition policies promise to cut the cost of
Alzheimer's disease prognosis by deciding, per patient, which scan to order,
but to our knowledge none has been compared with the others or with the
trivial baselines that any cost-saving claim must beat. We re-implemented
three published policies (an uncertainty-gated cascade, a staged evidential
method and a reinforcement-learning test-ordering policy) as nine
configurations under one leakage-free protocol, using 1,566 participants with
mild cognitive impairment from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging
Initiative (ADNI) and conversion outcomes at 12, 24 and 36 months. Against
random acquisition at the same expected cost, 25 of 27 configuration-horizon
pairs showed no significant difference after Holm correction under price-like
costs (one better, one worse), and 19 of 27 were dominated by a
patient-independent fixed rule costing no more. A fixed rule using expanded
structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) features and training on
partially observed participants (signature-pooled fixed acquisition, SPFA)
beat cost-matched random acquisition at its MRI-only operating point (area
under the receiver operating characteristic curve, AUC, +0.056 and +0.046 at 24 and 36 months; Holm-adjusted
$p=0.010$ and $0.016$) and showed no significant AUC difference from
acquiring all four modalities at one-third to one-tenth of the cost, except
for MRI-only at 36 months in development. The held-out test set, scored on
two successive data releases (the second is reported), is small and cannot
establish equivalence. Complete-case analysis reduced non-White
representation from 14.2\% to 5.9\%, making race-stratified fairness
auditing infeasible.

\section{Introduction}
Predicting whether a person with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) will
progress to Alzheimer's disease (AD) dementia is usually framed as a
prediction problem: assemble as many modalities as possible -- structural
magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) and amyloid
positron emission tomography (PET), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) assays --
and maximise
discrimination. In a clinic, it is also an economics problem. Amyloid-PET
is expensive and exposes the patient to radiation; a lumbar puncture is
invasive; MRI is comparatively cheap. Acquiring everything for everyone is
neither affordable nor necessary.

This has motivated a line of work on \emph{adaptive acquisition policies}:
models that decide, per patient, which modality to acquire next, typically
escalating only the cases the cheaper data leaves uncertain. Published
policies of this kind report substantial savings -- reductions of 50--90\%
in imaging use at maintained accuracy are claimed [1, 2, 3].

To our knowledge these claims have not been tested against each other, and,
more importantly, they have not been tested against the baselines any cost-saving claim must
clear. Two are essential. The first is \emph{random} acquisition: if
escalating a randomly chosen half of patients performs as well, the policy's
decisions carry no information, only its willingness to spend. The second
is a \emph{fixed} rule that acquires the same modalities for everyone: if a
rule that never looks at the patient matches the policy at equal or lower
cost, personalisation has bought nothing. To our knowledge, neither baseline
appears in the original reports.

This paper supplies that audit. The contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
\item A head-to-head benchmark of three published adaptive acquisition
policies, re-implemented under one leakage-free protocol, across three
prediction horizons and three acquisition-cost regimes, with paired
statistical comparison against random and fixed baselines.
\item Random-acquisition baselines swept over acquisition rate and matched
to each policy's expected cost, a cost-domination analysis quantifying how
often a learned policy is beaten by a patient-independent rule costing no
more, and a robustness sweep over predictor family, cost regime and random
seed.
\item A quantification of \emph{representation attrition}: the demographic
cost of the complete-case multimodal designs this literature relies on,
and its consequence for fairness auditing.
\end{enumerate}

We report a largely negative result, and one constructive consequence of
it. We are explicit throughout about which findings are new and which
reproduce established results in a new setting.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Adaptive acquisition policies for AD.} Three published methods are
audited here. Zheng et al. [1] gate amyloid-PET acquisition on the
predictive uncertainty of an MRI model, reporting an area under the receiver
operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.880 while saving 77\% of amyloid-PET
scans (23\% of patients escalated), against 0.905 for acquiring all
modalities. A staged evidential method [2] performs Dirichlet-based
evidential classification on cheap clinical data and escalates to MRI or
PET when uncertainty exceeds a learned threshold, fusing modality opinions
by Dempster-Shafer combination and reporting 50--90\% reductions in imaging
use. A reinforcement-learning (RL) formulation [3] treats diagnosis as sequential
decision-making, learning a policy that chooses which test to order next.
To our knowledge, none of the three reports a comparison against random
acquisition or against a cost-matched fixed rule, and none has been compared against the others.

\textbf{Active feature acquisition.} The broader machine-learning
literature on acquiring features under a budget is mature, with a recent
survey and benchmark [4, 5], and a formal evaluation framework for the
static setting [6]. Our finding is consistent with a
known and uncomfortable pattern in that literature: informed acquisition
strategies frequently fail to beat random selection. This has been
documented for unsupervised feature selection [7] and, in an
inflammatory bowel disease cohort, as a ``flat-to-negative lift over random''
for the leading planner when evaluated against clinical labels [8]. Our
contribution is not the discovery of that pattern but its demonstration for these specific,
named AD policies under a controlled protocol.

\textbf{Simple baselines in AD prediction.} That simple models are hard to
beat in this domain is likewise established. The TADPOLE challenge received
92 submissions from 33 teams and found that for ADAS-Cog13 forecasting no
submission was considerably better than the organisers' benchmark
mixed-effects model [9]. Our result extends that lesson from forecasting to acquisition.

\textbf{Structural features and incomplete data.} The constructive part of
our result rests on two established findings, and we claim novelty for
neither. First, the AD-signature cortical thickness measure predicts
progression better than hippocampal volume, and performed best among the
MRI and CSF markers compared by Dickerson and Wolk [10]. Second,
restricting analysis to complete cases wastes data: the incomplete-multimodal
learning literature is founded on precisely this observation [11]. What is
new here is the use of these two established techniques as the comparison
baseline against which adaptive policies are measured, which has not
previously been done.

\textbf{Methodological rigour.} Reported accuracies in this field are known
to be inflated by data leakage. A scoping review of 44 AD deep-learning
studies found that 68.2\% relied on ADNI and only 15.9\% performed any
external validation [12], and splitting by slice rather than by subject can
move reported accuracy by as much as 28 percentage points [12, 13]. Our protocol is designed
against these failure modes. Fairness in AD prediction models has been
examined for prediction accuracy [14], but not, to our knowledge, for
acquisition decisions.

\subsection{Cohort}
Data were drawn from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI)
database (adni.loni.usc.edu). Diagnosis, demographics, genotype, cognitive
assessments, FreeSurfer morphometry, PET summaries and CSF assays were
assembled from the source tables rather than a pre-merged file. All joins
use ADNI's harmonised visit code; the protocol-specific visit code is not
comparable across study phases and its use silently corrupts longitudinal
merges. Screening visits were coalesced into baseline where no baseline
record exists, with a genuine baseline record always taking precedence --
without this, 274 MCI participants whose diagnosis is recorded only at
screening are dropped, and dropped unevenly by phase.

Inclusion was MCI at the baseline visit, giving 1,566 participants from
3,762. Baseline predictors come only from the baseline-equivalent record
(the baseline visit or, where absent, the screening visit; for one FreeSurfer
table, a scan coded as month 3 that ADNI treats as baseline-equivalent), and
tables are joined on exact visit codes rather than nearest-date matching, so
predictors are limited to baseline-equivalent measurements.

\textbf{Outcome definition.} For a horizon of $h\in\{12,24,36\}$ months, a
participant is a converter (label 1) if any recorded visit at or before month
$h$ has a diagnosis of dementia, using the month implied by the harmonised
visit code; a conversion stays a conversion if the diagnosis later reverts.
A participant with no such visit is a non-converter (label 0) if their last
recorded visit is at or after month $h$, and is otherwise censored: no label
is assigned and they are excluded from that horizon rather than coded as
non-converters. No visit-window tolerance is applied. Participants who
return to normal cognition at any visit without a prior dementia diagnosis
are flagged as reverters; they are labelled 0 for classification but
counted separately in cohort reporting rather than merged into ``stable''.
Converter counts were 134, 252 and 313, and reverter counts 120, 113 and
110, at the three horizons. Table 1 traces the 24-month cohort to the final
analysis sets; the same participants can contribute to more than one
horizon.

\subsection{Acquirable modality blocks and costs}
Demographics, education, apolipoprotein E (APOE) genotype, Mini-Mental State
Examination (MMSE) and Clinical Dementia Rating Sum of Boxes (CDR-SB) are
treated as always available at zero acquisition cost: they are in hand
before any scan is ordered. Four blocks are acquirable in the benchmark:
MRI, FDG-PET, amyloid-PET and CSF. FDG, amyloid and tau PET are separate
scans with separate tracers and are therefore treated as separate
acquisitions rather than one ``PET'' block; conflating them misstates the
decision. Tau-PET and plasma biomarkers are defined in the feature code but
excluded from the benchmark: only 153--299 labelled participants hold tau-PET
(20--43 converters, depending on horizon), and only 19--122 hold a plasma
measurement, too few at any horizon. In the main benchmark every policy, including the
fixed rules, uses the same 12-measure structural MRI block described in
Section 3.5, and so do the robustness, clinical-utility, fairness and
horizon analyses of Sections 4.3 to 4.6. The customary three-feature block
(hippocampus, ventricles, intracranial volume) serves only as the ablation
reference.

Costs are relative units, not currency, and are deliberately swept across
three regimes rather than fixed: \emph{price-like} (MRI 1.0, FDG 2.0,
amyloid 5.0, CSF 1.5, roughly proportional to order-of-magnitude list
prices), \emph{flat} (all 1.0, isolating how many tests from which tests),
and \emph{burden} (MRI 1.0, FDG 3.0, amyloid 4.0, CSF 6.0, penalising the
invasive lumbar puncture and radiation-exposing tracers; these weights are
judgement-based). Acquiring all four modalities therefore costs 9.5, 4.0 and
14.0 units. Any conclusion that holds under only one regime is reported as
regime-dependent.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
Comparisons are made on the complete-case cohort for the four blocks, so
that every policy could in principle have acquired anything; otherwise a
policy that happens to prefer the most-available modality wins for the wrong
reason. Splits are at subject level and made before any preprocessing
statistic is computed, stratified jointly on outcome and scanner field
strength. Imputation and scaling are fitted inside each cross-validation
fold on that fold's training rows only. Each policy, including every
internal threshold, is refitted within each fold.

A test set was held out (a stratified 20\% of the complete-case cohort) and
left untouched through all development. All development figures come from
five-fold cross-validation (CV) on the remaining subjects.
\emph{Test-set protocol.} The analysis was completed and the test set scored
once, at the end, on the September 2026 ADNI release. The data were then
re-downloaded (October 2026 release, which changed the cohort slightly) and
the whole pipeline was re-run with unchanged models, operating points and test
procedure, and the test set was scored a second time. We report the October
results throughout; the two scorings differ by at most 0.011 AUC for any
model and horizon. Because the test set was scored on both releases, it is
not untouched with respect to the first, and the test results should be read
as two scorings of one fixed procedure, not as an independent replication.
The test set was not used to choose anything, and the post hoc checks of
Section 4.4 that look at it are descriptive.
Labelled subjects who lack at least one of the four modalities (483 at 24
months) are never evaluated: they form a training pool $P$ used only by the
proposed configuration (Section 3.5), and are disjoint from every
development fold and from the test set by construction. Confidence
intervals (CIs) are percentile intervals from 1,000 bootstrap resamples of
subjects.

\subsection{Policies compared}
Reference points are \emph{cheap-only} (never acquires), \emph{acquire-all}
(always acquires everything), \emph{fixed} rules acquiring a set prefix for
every patient (MRI; MRI then FDG), and a \emph{random} baseline in which each
of the four modalities is acquired independently with a given probability
(the acquisition rate). A single rate is not cost-matched, so the rate is
swept from 0 to 1 in steps of 0.1 and every rate is repeated over 50 random
seeds, with an independent random draw for every patient. The expected cost
at rate $r$ is $r$ times the cost of acquiring everything (4.75 price-like
units at $r=0.5$); the \emph{realised} cost is the empirical mean over the
simulated draws (4.73, 4.73 and 4.72 at $r=0.5$ at 12, 24 and 36 months).
Table 2 reports realised costs at 24 months and Table 3 the three-horizon
mean. Each policy is compared with random acquisition at the same expected
cost (Section 3.6). No public code exists for any of the three published policies, so each was
re-implemented from its description, giving the nine configurations listed in
Table 2; results should therefore be read as our implementation of each
mechanism rather than a reproduction of the original numbers. Table 3 and
Figure 3 show one representative configuration of each policy: the
uncertainty cascade at a target escalation of 53.8\%, the staged evidential method's full
ladder with the shared predictor, and the RL policy at cost weight 0.01. The
calibration, decision-curve and fairness analyses (Figures 7 and 11) use the
RL policy at cost weight 0.05.

Prediction is delegated to a shared logistic-regression model bank for all
policies, so that differences between policies are differences in the
\emph{acquisition decision} rather than in predictor strength. For the
evidential method [2], whose predictor follows evidential deep learning
[15], this required an additional variant: its neural predictor is weaker
than logistic regression at this sample size, and reporting only the
faithful version would penalise its decision rule for its predictor. Both
are reported.

\subsection{Proposed algorithm: signature-pooled fixed acquisition}
The audit motivates a deliberately simple alternative, which we call
\emph{signature-pooled fixed acquisition} (SPFA). SPFA is not a new decision
rule. Because learned rules did not beat fixed ones, SPFA keeps the
acquisition decision trivial -- every patient receives the same pre-specified
modality set $A$ -- and changes only what the predictor for that set is built
on. Two ingredients, both established, are combined:

\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
\item \textbf{Signature features.} The customary three-feature MRI block
(hippocampus, ventricles and intracranial volume, ICV) is replaced by 12
FreeSurfer measures from regions affected early in AD, motivated by the
AD-signature literature [10] rather than reproducing its published composite:
Hippocampus, Amygdala, Entorhinal (cortical volume and mean thickness),
Fusiform, MidTemporal, InfTemporal, ParaHippocampal, Precuneus and
PosteriorCingulate (cortical volumes), Ventricles and ICV. Nine regions give
ten regional measures, plus the two global ones. Because the main benchmark
gives every policy this same block (Section 3.2), SPFA differs from the fixed
rules of Table 3 only in ingredient (ii).
\item \textbf{Pooled state training.} The model for acquisition state
$S=\mathrm{CHEAP}\cup A$ needs only the blocks in $S$, so it is fitted on
every labelled subject who holds those blocks rather than on the
complete-case cohort alone [11].
\end{enumerate}

Algorithm 1 gives the training procedure and the deployment step is
described below it; Figure 2 summarises both. Three properties follow from the design.
\emph{Cost is fixed and known in advance}: $\sum_{b\in A}c_b$ for every
patient. \emph{Acquisition disparity is zero by construction}, since the
decision never consults the patient. \emph{Leakage is excluded by construction}: the
pool $P$ is disjoint from the complete-case cohort, and hence from every
development fold, inner validation fold and the test set, so pooled rows only
ever add training data. Imputation and scaling are fitted on $T_S$ only, and
the regularisation strength $C$ is selected by inner CV on development rows
alone. For the held-out test, each operating point is refitted once on all
development subjects plus $P_S$ and scored (Section 3.2). The operating points $A=\{\mathrm{MRI}\}$ and $A=\{\mathrm{MRI},\mathrm{FDG}\}$
were fixed in advance and were not chosen using the test set.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{fig_algorithm_flow.png}

\textbf{Figure 2.} The proposed SPFA procedure: training (top) and

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{SPFA training for acquisition state $S=\mathrm{CHEAP}\cup A$}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Training rows $D_{tr}$ (development subjects outside the
evaluation fold); pool $P$ (labelled subjects lacking at least one modality,
disjoint from all development and test subjects); acquisition set $A$;
regularisation grid $\mathcal{C}$
\Ensure Fitted risk model $f_S$
\State $P_S \gets \{p\in P : p \text{ holds every block in } S\}$
\State $T_S \gets D_{tr}\cup P_S$ \Comment{pooled training rows}
\If{$|T_S|<n_{\min}$ \textbf{or} $T_S$ has one class}
\State $T_S \gets D_{tr}$ \Comment{fall back to complete cases (in every fit)}
\EndIf
\ForAll{$C\in\mathcal{C}$} \Comment{inner 3-fold CV on $D_{tr}$ only}
\State $\mathrm{AUC}(C)\gets$ mean AUC over inner folds $V\subset D_{tr}$ of
a model fitted with $C$ on $(D_{tr}\setminus V)\cup P_S$ and scored on $V$
\EndFor
\State $C^\ast \gets \arg\max_{C}\mathrm{AUC}(C)$
\State Fit median imputer and standard scaler on $T_S$ (features of $S$ only)
\State Fit $L_2$ logistic regression $f_S$ on $T_S$ with $C^\ast$
\State \Return $f_S$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Deployment is a single step: acquire the fixed set $A$ for every patient
and return $\hat p=f_S(x)$ at cost $\kappa=\sum_{b\in A}c_b$.
Honest performance estimation uses nested cross-validation: the outer loop
measures AUC, and $C^\ast$ is re-selected inside every outer fold, so the
reported figure is never one that anything was tuned on. The measured
selection optimism of the non-nested estimate was +0.0016 AUC.

\subsection{Statistics}
Differences between policies are assessed by paired bootstrap on pooled
out-of-fold predictions for the same subjects (1,000 resamples of subjects,
two-sided 95\% percentile intervals; a difference is called significant when
the interval excludes zero), which is considerably more sensitive than
comparing independent confidence intervals. Each policy is compared with
\emph{random acquisition at the same expected cost}: for a policy whose mean
cost is $c$ under a given cost regime, the reference is random acquisition
at rate $c$ divided by the cost of acquiring all four modalities, averaged
over 50 seeds and scored on the same subjects and the same cross-validation
folds. The paired difference carries a 95\% percentile interval and a two-sided
bootstrap $p$-value from 10,000 subject resamples (the $p$-value is
$(k+1)/(B+1)$, so it cannot fall below $10^{-4}$). The \emph{comparison
family} is the 13 policies that differ from random acquisition at their own
cost, within each horizon and cost regime: the nine published configurations,
fixed MRI, fixed MRI+FDG and the two SPFA operating points. Cheap-only and
acquire-all are excluded, since random acquisition at rate 0 and 1 is
identical to them. Holm adjustment is applied within each family of 13. We
report the unadjusted interval and the Holm-adjusted $p$-value separately and
call a difference \emph{significant after adjustment} only when the adjusted
$p<0.05$; ``significant'' without qualification never refers to the unadjusted
interval. Comparisons with acquire-all are paired bootstrap intervals without
adjustment and are descriptive.

The matching is by \emph{expected} cost, not a strict fixed budget. Random
acquisition draws a different cost for every patient, so a policy whose mean
cost is $c$ is compared with random acquisition whose mean cost over 50 seeds
is within 0.03, 0.01 and 0.05 units of $c$ (price-like, flat, burden; largest
deviation over all policies and horizons), and whose seed-to-seed SD of mean
cost is at most 0.23 units. Per-patient cost varies: at the matched rate its
SD is 0.6 to 2.8 units under price-like costs, against 0.5 to 3.0 for the
adaptive policies at their own cost, so the two spend with comparable spread.
The same procedure is applied separately under each regime, with that regime's
cost weights and its own matched rate. Acquisition-rate
disparities are tested with a bootstrap over subgroups and corrected for
multiplicity using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure [16]; with dozens of
(policy, modality) comparisons, uncorrected significance is not evidence.
Calibration is reported as slope, intercept and Brier score, and clinical
utility as net benefit across threshold probabilities [17].

\section{Results and Discussion}

\subsection{Adaptive policies do not outperform cost-matched random acquisition}
Table 3 gives cross-validated AUC by policy and horizon on the development
set, and Figure 3 plots the same results as a cost-AUC frontier. Differences
between policies are small and their ranking changes between horizons.
Random acquisition is not a single number, because its AUC depends on how
much it spends. Across the rate sweep it rises smoothly from the cheap-only
value (0.816, 0.810 and 0.793 at 12, 24 and 36 months) to the acquire-all
value (0.862, 0.893 and 0.898), reaching 0.846, 0.870 and 0.868 at rate 0.5
(Figure 4). Its seed-to-seed SD is 0.004--0.019 at intermediate rates, and
the subject-bootstrap interval around the seed-averaged AUC has a half-width
of 0.03--0.06, which is the real uncertainty in the baseline; the
acquire-all AUC lies inside the rate-0.5 interval at every horizon. (A
single draw of one random policy can therefore land well above or below the
mean, which is why a single-rate, single-seed baseline is not informative.)

Compared with random acquisition at the same expected cost, \textbf{adaptive
selection gave no consistent gain}. We report unadjusted and Holm-adjusted
results separately (Section 3.6). Of the 27 configuration-horizon pairs for
the published policies under price-like costs, \emph{unadjusted}, 22 had a
paired 95\% interval including zero, 2 were better and 3 were worse.
\emph{After Holm adjustment}, 25 of 27 showed no significant difference: one
was better (the RL policy at cost weight 0.05 at 12 months, AUC +0.040, 95\%
CI +0.016 to +0.066, adjusted $p=0.014$, which did not recur at 24 or 36
months) and one was worse (the staged evidential method's full ladder with
its own neural predictor at 12 months, $-0.169$, adjusted $p=0.003$). The
other three unadjusted differences did not survive adjustment: losses of the
evidential method's MRI-then-amyloid variant with its own predictor at 12 and
24 months (adjusted $p=0.11$ and $0.10$) and a gain of the 25\%-escalation
uncertainty cascade at 36 months (+0.028, 95\% CI +0.002 to +0.053, adjusted
$p=0.37$). All three unadjusted losses were evidential variants using that
method's own neural predictor, which is weaker than logistic regression at
this sample size (Section 3.4).
Under flat costs, 1 pair was better and 3 worse unadjusted, and after
adjustment none was better and one worse (the same evidential full ladder);
the RL gain at 12 months is not significant after adjustment under flat costs
(adjusted $p=0.079$). Under burden costs, 4 were better and 2 worse
unadjusted, and after adjustment 2 were better (the same RL gain, adjusted
$p=0.026$, and the 25\%-escalation cascade at 36 months, adjusted $p=0.035$)
and one worse. The gains therefore depend on the cost regime, and neither
recurs across horizons.

The reference rules tell a different story. At their own cost, fixed MRI and
fixed MRI+FDG exceeded random acquisition at matched cost by +0.009 to +0.036
AUC, with unadjusted intervals excluding zero at 24 and 36 months for both
rules (for example fixed MRI+FDG at 24 months, +0.034, 95\% CI +0.011 to
+0.058; adjusted $p=0.058$);
and SPFA, which uses the same fixed acquisition rule with a better predictor,
did so, unadjusted, at all horizons for MRI+FDG and, after adjustment, at 24
and 36 months for MRI-only (Section 4.4). Cheap-only and acquire-all coincide with
random at rates 0 and 1 by construction. The conclusion that survives is
therefore narrower than ``nothing beats random'': \emph{choosing per patient
adds nothing to what the same budget buys at random, whereas a fixed rule
with good inputs does beat both}. Random acquisition is also a stronger
baseline than a single rate suggests: it costs more than most published
configurations at its default rate (Table 2), so the matched comparison is
the fair test, and the domination analysis in the next section is its
complement, asking whether a patient-independent rule matches the policy at
no greater cost.

Figure 5a shows what each policy actually orders. The uncertainty cascade
sends every patient to MRI and roughly half (55\%) onward to amyloid-PET, so
its mean load is about 1.5 modalities. The staged evidential method acquires
each of the four modalities for about half of patients (about 1.9 modalities
per patient), a profile close to that of random acquisition at rate 0.5
(2.0). At cost weight 0.05 the RL policy orders MRI (40\%) and CSF (28\%) most
often, FDG sometimes (21\%) and amyloid-PET rarely (4\%); at cost weight 0.01
it orders about 2.2 modalities per patient (MRI 67\%, FDG 64\%, CSF 62\%,
amyloid-PET 31\%). Policies that spend
like random acquisition and perform no better than it have, in effect,
learned to spend rather than to select.


\subsection{Learned policies are dominated by fixed rules}
A stronger test than AUC alone is domination: is a learned policy beaten by a
patient-independent rule that costs no more? Under price-like costs,
\textbf{19 of 27 configuration-horizon pairs were dominated} (4, 7 and 8 of
the nine configurations at 12, 24 and 36 months), by fixed MRI+FDG (cost
3.00) in 9 cases, by cheap-only in 5 and by fixed MRI in 5. Figure 5b repeats
the count under each cost regime.

\par\vspace{3pt}

Domination depends partly on the cost regime, and we report it as such.
Under flat costs 12 of the 27 pairs remain dominated (3, 3 and 6 at the
three horizons) and under burden costs 16 (4, 6 and 6); the 12 flat-cost
pairs are dominated under all three regimes. At 24 months the uncertainty
cascade escapes domination under flat and burden costs, and the staged
evidential method with the shared predictor under flat costs. The comparison
with cost-matched random acquisition in Section 4.1 is made separately under
each regime and gives the same qualitative answer under all three.

\subsection{Robustness}
Two further checks probe the negative result; both give every policy the
same 12-measure MRI block as the main benchmark. Replacing the shared
logistic regression with gradient boosting did not rescue the published
policies: at 24 months two of the three representative configurations (the
uncertainty cascade and RL at cost weight 0.05) were dominated, and the
staged evidential method was not (0.868 at cost 4.6, against 0.855 for fixed
MRI+FDG at 3.0 and 0.881 for acquire-all at 9.5).
Gradient boosting lowered AUC for every policy, by 0.010 (evidential) to
0.055 (RL) and by 0.031 for fixed MRI+FDG (Figure 6b), so logistic regression
was the stronger predictor at this sample size. Across random seeds 0, 7 and
42, per-policy AUC standard deviations (SDs) were 0.004--0.014 (Figure 6a);
the ranking shifted between seeds, but acquire-all ranked first and fixed MRI
and cheap-only last at every seed, and the best published policy ranked
second at one seed and below fixed MRI+FDG at the other two.

Decision-curve analysis extended the comparison to clinical utility
(Figure 7a). Every strategy delivered a clear net benefit over treating
everyone at thresholds of 0.10 and above, and no published policy led
consistently: the highest net benefit came from acquire-all at four of the
ten thresholds (0.05, 0.15, 0.20 and 0.30), fixed MRI+FDG at 0.10, the
uncertainty cascade at 0.25, and SPFA with MRI+FDG at 0.35--0.50. Where a
published policy exceeded the best reference strategy (thresholds 0.25, 0.35
and 0.40) it did so by at most 0.005. Calibration slopes did not favour the
published policies either: cheap-only was the best calibrated reference
(slope 0.828), random acquisition scored 0.780, the three published policies
ranged from 0.733 to 0.756, and acquire-all was the worst (0.725); SPFA was
closest to ideal (1.080 and 1.104). Figure 7b shows reliability curves for
the reference strategies and SPFA.

obtainable directly. Table 4 gives the nested cross-validated AUC of SPFA,
which combines the expanded MRI block [10] with training each acquisition
state on every participant who holds it rather than on complete cases only
[11]. Neither ingredient is novel; their use as the baseline against which
adaptive policies are measured is. Cost ratios refer to acquire-all (9.5
price-like units): 9.5 for MRI-only and 3.2 for MRI+FDG.

only was better by +0.032, +0.056 and +0.046 AUC at 12, 24 and 36 months; the
24- and 36-month intervals excluded zero (+0.026 to +0.089 and +0.018 to

survived adjustment. The adjustment
matters: the strongest claim these data support is for the MRI-only operating
point, with the MRI+FDG point suggestive.

An ablation separates the two ingredients (Figure 8), relative to the
three-feature block trained on complete cases. At the 24-month horizon, the
combination improves the MRI-only state by +0.034 AUC
(95\% CI +0.007 to +0.063). Pooled training alone gives +0.019 (+0.004 to
+0.036), whose interval also excludes zero, and the expanded features alone
+0.013 ($-0.011$ to +0.039), whose interval includes zero, so the significant
part of the gain at this state is attributable to pooling. At richer
acquisition states (MRI+FDG or more) the changes are within noise (at most
+0.007) and slightly negative at the amyloid state ($-0.009$, interval
including zero), so the benefit is concentrated at the cheap end of the
frontier, which is where it matters for cost. Because the main benchmark
already gives every policy the expanded block, the gap between SPFA and the
fixed rules in Table 3 reflects pooling alone: +0.021 AUC for MRI-only at 24
months (95\% CI +0.001 to +0.042), significant only at the margin.

Three cautions attach to the test figures. The test sets are small (9, 17
and 22 converters), so ``not significantly worse'' is weak evidence of
equivalence: the intervals are compatible with losses of up to 0.186 AUC for
MRI-only at 36 months. For the two SPFA operating points, test AUC differed
from the nested development estimate by $-0.005$ to $+0.072$ (largest for
MRI-only at 12 months: 0.944 against 0.872), and the 12-month test set
appears easier overall: all five models scored 0.066 to 0.099 above their
development estimates, including cheap-only (+0.099). Two post hoc,
descriptive checks (nothing was tuned on them) put this in context. First, we
drew 20,000 random subsets of the development out-of-fold predictions with
the same size and number of converters as each test set. The standard
deviation of AUC across such subsets is 0.04--0.06 at 12 months and
0.03--0.05 at 24 and 36 months, and the observed 12-month test AUCs fall at
the 92nd to 99th percentile of that distribution (24 and 36 months: 41st to
95th, with acquire-all at 36 months the highest at 94.5th); the five models
share the same test subjects, so these percentiles are not independent. A
12-month gap of this size is therefore unusual but not implausible for nine
converters. Second, test and development subjects were similar at baseline:
standardised mean differences in age, education, MMSE, CDR-SB, APOE4 and
hippocampal and ventricular volume were at most 0.23 in magnitude, and
outcome prevalence matched (9.0\% against 8.9\%, 18.3\% against 18.5\%, and
25.7\% against 26.2\% at 12, 24 and 36 months). We therefore attribute the
differences to small-sample variation rather than a real gain, but cannot
exclude other causes; the nested figures in Table 4 are the ones to quote as
expected performance. As a check on leakage, test subjects are excluded from
the training pool by construction and the split asserts that no subject
appears in two partitions; operating points, feature blocks and the
regularisation grid were fixed before the test set was scored, and it was
scored at the end on two releases (Section 3.2).

The practical consequence is that a race-stratified fairness audit of these
policies is not currently feasible: the 366-participant audit cohort
contains 9 Black, 9 Hispanic and 4 Asian participants, with no converters in
any of these groups, so neither acquisition rates nor error rates can be
estimated with any precision. This is a limitation of the data, not a
finding about the policies: reduced representation shows that these groups
cannot be audited, not that the policies treat them unfairly. On sex, the only
adequately powered dimension (213 male and 153 female participants), no
acquisition disparity survived multiplicity correction. Two gaps reached
$p=0.050$ uncorrected -- the evidential method ordering FDG-PET 10.0
percentage points less often for male participants, and the RL policy (cost
weight 0.05) ordering 0.23 more modalities per male participant -- but
neither survived Benjamini-Hochberg across the comparison family, and they are
reported as suggestive only (Figure 11). We note that fixed policies cannot
exhibit acquisition disparity at all, since they do not consult the patient.

\subsection{Horizon dependence}
A secondary analysis asked whether modality value depends on the prediction
horizon, which would motivate horizon-conditioned policies. This analysis
uses the same 12-measure MRI block as the main benchmark. On a fixed subject
set, amyloid-PET and CSF showed a significant increase in incremental value
from 12 to 36 months (+0.049, 95\% CI +0.021 to +0.080, and +0.040, 95\% CI
+0.010 to +0.068); MRI and FDG did not (tau-PET, flagged underpowered, also
increased). A horizon-conditioned policy built on this effect did not
outperform horizon-swapped controls, indicating the effect is real as a
measurement but not exploitable as an acquisition rule at this sample size.
Figure 12 shows the incremental value of each modality by horizon (a) and on
subjects matched to each modality, including all four together (b).

\subsection{Limitations}
The three policies are re-implementations from published descriptions, as
no public code exists for any of them; a negative result about a
re-implementation is weaker evidence than one about original code, and we
state this plainly. Sample sizes are modest -- 67 converters in the
24-month development cohort and 17 in its test set -- so several
non-significant comparisons are underpowered, and ``not distinguishable''
should not be read as ``equivalent''. Random acquisition is matched to each policy
by expected cost, not as a strict fixed budget: the matched rate is derived
from the policy's mean cost, and a policy's per-patient cost can differ from
random acquisition's even when the means agree (Section 3.6). Comparisons
against cost-matched random are Holm-adjusted within each horizon and cost
regime (families of 13), but not across horizons and regimes together, and
comparisons against acquire-all are unadjusted. In the
proposed configuration, different acquisition states are fitted on different,
overlapping subject sets, so part of the gain may reflect which subjects
each state was trained on, and one FreeSurfer table treats a month-3 scan as
baseline-equivalent. The held-out test set was scored on two ADNI data
releases (Section 3.2) with unchanged code, so it cannot be treated as
untouched with respect to the first. Acquisition costs are relative units
whose absolute values are contestable, which is why every cost-dependent
conclusion is reported across three regimes. ADNI's missingness is
protocol-driven rather than random, so any retrospective estimate of what
could have been skipped is a simulation and not a prospective guarantee.
MRI morphometry pools several FreeSurfer pipeline versions, and the results
come from a single cohort without external validation.

\section{Conclusion}
Three published adaptive modality-acquisition policies for Alzheimer's
disease prognosis were re-implemented and audited under a common
leakage-free protocol across three prediction horizons and three cost
regimes. Against random acquisition at the same expected cost, 25 of 27
configuration-horizon pairs showed no significant difference after Holm
correction under price-like costs (one better, one worse), and 19 of 27 were dominated by a
patient-independent fixed rule costing no more under price-like costs (12
under every cost regime tested). For most configurations, then, the cost
savings these methods report do not appear to derive from personalisation: a
rule that ignores the patient captures them as well, and so does spending the
same budget at random. The one significant gain, the RL policy at cost weight
0.05 at 12 months, did not recur at 24 or 36 months.

Consistent with this, SPFA, built from two existing ideas -- an expanded set
of structural MRI features and training on partially observed participants --
beat cost-matched random acquisition at its MRI-only operating point at 24 and
36 months, and showed no significant AUC difference from acquiring four
modalities at about one-third to one-tenth of the acquisition cost, with the
caveats that its cheapest operating point was significantly lower at the
36-month horizon in development and that the small test set cannot establish
equivalence.

Finally, complete-case multimodal analysis reduces non-White representation
in this cohort from 14.2\% to 5.9\%, and recent recruitment gains are not
yet visible to conversion studies because those participants lack
follow-up. Race-stratified fairness auditing of acquisition policies on
the current complete-case ADNI cohort is therefore not feasible, a constraint the field should
state explicitly rather than rediscover per study.

Future work should test these policies on prospective or more diverse
cohorts, and should evaluate acquisition methods against random and
cost-matched fixed baselines as a default, since neither appears in the
original reports.
